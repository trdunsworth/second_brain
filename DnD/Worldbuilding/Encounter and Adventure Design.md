# Encounter and Adventure Design

This is where the other ten notes become sessions. The SRD supplies a complete loop: **three pillars → a D20 Test → an action → a consequence**, plus a fully worked **XP budget method** (SRD pp. 202–203), eleven travel terrains (SRD p. 192), traps with level bands (SRD pp. 199–201), and environmental/fear/poison rules (SRD pp. 195–197). What it does *not* supply — adventuring-day budgets, morale, random tables — is listed at the bottom.

## The loop (SRD p. 5)

> [!src] SRD p. 5 — Rhythm of Play
> "…adventurers are talking to a noble, exploring a ruin, or fighting a dragon."

- **Three pillars:** social, exploration, combat (SRD p. 5).
- **D20 Tests** are the shared engine — "the GM might ask the player to roll a die to help determine what happens. Describing the results often leads to another decision point, which brings the game back to step 1" (SRD p. 5).
- **Actions** govern what you can do at once: "You can take only one action at a time" — including **Influence** in social scenes and **Search** to read body language, but not both in the same turn (SRD p. 10).
- **Checks** (SRD p. 6), **proficiency** (SRD p. 8), the **skills table** (SRD p. 9) — Arcana, History, Religion, Nature, Survival and the rest, each with one line of use.
- Then the three scene types: **Social Interaction** (SRD p. 10), **Exploration** (SRD p. 11), **Hazards and Travel** (SRD p. 12), **Combat** (SRD p. 13).

Design consequence: every session should be able to answer *"which pillar is carrying this scene?"* — and the SRD's rules are written so that the same question (open a door, get a noble to talk, cross a valley) can be answered by any pillar.

**The three steps (SRD p. 202):**

1. **Choose a difficulty.**
   - *Low* — "one or two scary moments," no casualties; as a rough guideline a single monster whose CR equals the party's level is a low-difficulty challenge for four characters.
   - *Moderate* — "could go badly for the adventurers"; weaker characters might go down; slim chance of a death.
   - *High* — "could be lethal for one or more characters"; needs smart tactics and luck.
2. **Determine your XP budget:** multiply the table value by the number of characters.
3. **Spend your budget:** deduct each creature's XP; "spend as much of your XP budget as you can without going over. It's OK if you have a few unspent XP left over."

**SRD's own worked examples (SRD pp. 202–203):**

- Low, four level 1 characters → 50 × 4 = **200 XP**: 1 Bugbear Warrior (200) *or* 2 Giant Wasps (100 each) *or* 6 Giant Rats (25 each).
- Moderate, five level 3 characters → 225 × 5 = **1,125 XP**: 2 Druids + 9 Stirges (1,125) *or* 1 Wight + 1 Warhorse Skeleton + 6 Skeletons (1,100).
- High, six level 15 characters → 7,800 × 6 = **46,800 XP**: 2 Adult Red Dragons (18,000 each) + 2 Fire Giants (5,000 each) = 46,000.

**Make it interesting (SRD p. 202):** changes in elevation (crates, ledges, balconies), defensive positions ("hard-to-reach locations… force characters who normally attack at range to move around"), mixed monster groups ("just like characters with different classes and origins"), and **reasons to move** (chandeliers, kegs of gunpowder or oil, rolling stone traps).

**Troubleshooting (SRD p. 203):**

| Problem | The SRD's fix |
| --- | --- |
| Too many creatures (>2 per character) | Include **fragile** creatures that can be defeated quickly — especially at levels 1–2 |
| Encounter too easy / too hard | Have creatures **flee**, or add **reinforcements** |
| CR 0 creatures in numbers | "use swarms instead" |
| Too many stat blocks to run | "more than two or three stat blocks for a single encounter can be daunting" |
| A creature above the party's level | It "might deal enough damage with a single action to take out one or more characters" (an Ogre, CR 2, can kill a level 1 Wizard with one blow) |
| A feature lower-level PCs can't overcome | Consider not adding that monster at all |

> [!tip] The party is not the only variable
> Elevation, cover, defenders' position, mixed roles, and a reason to move are **free difficulty** — they cost no XP and no balance risk. Exhaust them before you add a fifth creature.

## Travel, terrain, and the journey

### Pace math (SRD p. 192)

- `Miles per hour = Speed ÷ 10`
- `Miles per day (Normal pace) = miles per hour × number of hours traveled (typically 8)`
- `Fast pace = miles per day × 1⅓ (round down)` · `Slow pace = miles per day × 2/3 (round down)`
- **Good roads** raise the maximum pace one step (Slow → Normal, Normal → Fast).
- **Slower travelers:** if any member's Speed is reduced to half or less, the group moves at a **Slow** pace.
- **Extended travel:** beyond 8 hours, each additional hour requires a Constitution save — **DC 10 plus 1 per hour past 8** — or gain 1 Exhaustion level.
- **Flying / ignoring Difficult Terrain:** may travel at Fast pace regardless of terrain.
- **Vehicles:** use the vehicle's speed in miles per hour; you don't choose a travel pace.

### The Travel Terrain table (SRD p. 192)

| Terrain | Maximum Pace | Encounter Distance | Foraging DC | Navigation DC | Search DC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Arctic | Fast* | 6d6 × 10 feet | 20 | 10 | 10 |
| Coastal | Normal | 2d10 × 10 feet | 10 | 5 | 15 |
| Desert | Normal | 6d6 × 10 feet | 20 | 10 | 10 |
| Forest | Normal | 2d8 × 10 feet | 10 | 15 | 15 |
| Grassland | Fast | 6d6 × 10 feet | 15 | 5 | 15 |
| Hill | Normal | 2d10 × 10 feet | 15 | 10 | 15 |
| Mountain | Slow | 4d10 × 10 feet | 20 | 15 | 20 |
| Swamp | Slow | 2d8 × 10 feet | 10 | 15 | 20 |
| Underdark | Normal | 2d6 × 10 feet | 20 | 10 | 20 |
| Urban | Normal | 2d6 × 10 feet | 20 | 15 | 15 |
| Waterborne | Special† | 6d6 × 10 feet | 15 | 10 | 15 |

\* Appropriate equipment (such as skis) is necessary to keep up a Fast pace in Arctic terrain. † Depends on the vehicle (SRD p. 101).

**This table is five rules in one line per terrain.** The *encounter distance* column rolls up how far apart you are when something happens — combine it with the terrain's Search DC and you have a wilderness scene in one die roll.

### Light and obscured areas (SRD p. 11)

- **Lightly Obscured** (Dim Light, patchy fog, moderate foliage): Disadvantage on Wisdom (Perception) checks that rely on sight.
- **Heavily Obscured** (darkness, heavy fog, dense foliage): you have the **Blinded** condition while trying to see through it.
- Bright Light / Dim Light / Darkness categories are defined there too — this is your dungeon- and night-lighting toolkit.

### Environment (SRD pp. 195–196)

**Environmental Effects:** Deep Water · Extreme Cold · Extreme Heat · Heavy Precipitation · High Altitude · Slippery Ice — each with its own rule text. Cold also ties to gear: "While wrapped in a blanket, you have Advantage on saving throws against extreme cold" (SRD p. 96).

> [!src] SRD p. 196 — Fear and Mental Stress
> "Adventurers tend to be less susceptible to fear and mental stress than common folk… If you plan to use any of these rules, discuss them with your players at the start of the campaign."
>
> **Sample Fear DCs:** DC 10 — a harmless yet terrifying apparition from a sarcophagus · DC 15 — an illusory manifestation of that character's worst fears · DC 20 — a portal to the Abyss opens.
>
> **Sample Mental Stress Effects:** DC 10, 1d6 Psychic — hallucinogenic substance · DC 15, 3d6 Psychic — touching a fiendish idol · DC 20, 9d6 Psychic — a magical trap flings the character **into the Far Realm** until the end of their next turn.

Mental stress is emulated with **Psychic damage**; Wisdom save is typical, Intelligence or Charisma sometimes; on a success, half damage is at your discretion. Prolonged effects are yours to design.

### Poison as a scene (SRD p. 197)

> [!src] SRD p. 197 — Purchasing Poison
> "In some settings, laws prohibit the possession and use of poison, but an illicit dealer or unscrupulous apothecary might keep a hidden stash. Characters with criminal contacts might be able to acquire poison easily. Other characters might have to make expensive inquiries and pay bribes."

That paragraph is a full social encounter in three sentences: a law ([[Nations and Politics Template]]), a contact ([[NPC Framework]]), and a price ([[Economy and Downtime]]).

## Traps (SRD pp. 199–201)

> [!src] SRD p. 199 — Traps
> "Traps should be used sparingly, lest they lose their charm… The best traps are fleeting distractions that skilled characters can overcome in a short amount of time or deadly puzzles that require quick thinking and teamwork. Traps that are undetectable and inescapable are rarely fun."

**Parts of a trap:** *Severity and Levels* (nuisance or deadly, for stated levels) · *Trigger* · *Duration* · *Detect and Disarm* (via Search or Study, with DCs) · *At Higher Levels* scaling.

**The six sample traps, all level 1–4:**

| Trap | Severity | Trigger | Detect & Disarm |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Collapsing Roof | Deadly | trip wire | DC 11 Wisdom (Perception); cut or avoid, no check |
| Falling Net | Nuisance | trip wire | DC 11 Wisdom (Perception); DC 10 STR (Athletics) to free; set it with DC 13 DEX (Sleight of Hand), 10 minutes |
| Fire-Casting Statue | Deadly | pressure plate | DC 10 Perception (glyph) + DC 15 Arcana to read it; DC 15 Perception for the plate |
| Hidden Pit | Nuisance | pit lid | DC 15 Intelligence (Investigation); wedge with an Iron Spike |
| Poisoned Darts | Deadly | pressure plate | DC 15 Perception (holes or plate); plug with wax/cloth |
| Poisoned Needle | Nuisance | wrong key / failed disarm | — |

**Scaling tables (SRD pp. 199–201)** run on level bands — e.g. Collapsing Roof: 11 (2d10) damage, DC 13 at levels 1–4 → 22 (4d10), DC 15 at 5–10 → 55 (10d10), DC 17 at 11–16 → 99 (18d10), DC 19 at 17–20. The Hidden Pit scales depth (30 / 60 / 120 feet) and falling damage instead.

> [!tip] Reuse these as *worldbuilding*
> A trap's "set the trap" rule (Thieves' Tools, 10 minutes, DC 13) means traps are *manufactured* — somebody in your [[Settlements and Locations Template]] sells them, or outlaws them.

## Curses as encounters (SRD p. 193)

Three forms with rules attached:

- **Bestow Curse** — "The simplest curses"; a 1-minute curse equates to a level 3 spell, one lasting until dispelled equates to a level 9 spell.
- **Cursed Creatures / Cursed Magic Items** — some monsters and items are cursed by origin; most identification "fails to reveal such a curse," and attunement to a cursed item can't be ended voluntarily until the curse is broken (SRD pp. 193, 206).
- **Narrative Curses** — "a creature's violation of a taboo warrants supernatural punishment, such as **breaking a vow, defiling a tomb, or murdering an innocent**. Such a curse can have any effects you design."
  - The afflicted "should know why they're being punished and be able to learn how to end their curse, likely by symbolically righting the wrong they committed."
  - Remove Curse's effect on it is *your* call — "the spell might merely suppress the effects."
- **Environmental Curses** — e.g. **Demonic Possession** (Abyss/desecrated locations): DC 15 Charisma save or possessed; on any **roll of 1 on a D20 Test** the entity takes control; end-of-turn DC 15 Cha save to regain control; after a Long Rest, DC 15 Cha save to end; *Dispel Evil and Good* or any curse-removing magic also ends it.

## Confirmed [GAP]s in this area

1. **No adventuring-day or daily XP budget.** The SRD budgets *encounters* (SRD p. 202) and never budgets a day of them.
2. **No morale.** 0 hits for `morale`; "Running a Monster" (SRD p. 255) covers tactics and gear only.
3. **No random encounter tables** by terrain or time of day — the Travel Terrain table gives you an *encounter distance*, not an encounter (SRD p. 192).
4. **No treasure-hoard tables** (SRD p. 204 describes hoards but gives no roll table).
5. **No random dungeon or site generator.**
6. **No weather roll tables** — the environmental effects are fixed rules, not a weather cycle (SRD pp. 195–196).
7. **No rules for the other two pillars' "budgets."** Social and exploration scenes have procedures (SRD pp. 10–12) but no difficulty-by-level math comparable to SRD p. 202.
8. **No chase, mass-combat, or siege rules.**
